"Non-State Actors, Sub-Conventional Warfare, and India-Pakistan Nuclear Crisis Stability/Instability." March 2024.

Non-state violence is complex and ranges from lone actors to state-armed proxy groups. The use of proxy fighters is problematic for international peace and security as it assumes that all violence is state-sponsored. Usually, states engage in proxy wars by hiding behind militant/terrorist organizations. At the official level, no state seems to accept the ownership of such organizations. Covertly, however, it often transpires that states fund, train, and patronize them. In many cases, the states themselves create such organizations clandestinely. The political, economic, military, and strategic issues between nuclear-armed India and Pakistan have their roots in the region’s various geopolitical and geostrategic realities, which exacerbate the tensions between the two. Jammu and Kashmir region is the main dispute to which both the countries lay claim has become the bone of contention for all crises and wars. The Line of Control (LoC) that divides the disputed territory continues to be tense, and the exchange of artillery is a routine. <u>The enduring </u>==<u>conflict</u>==<u> between India and Pakistan </u>==<u>over </u>==Kashmir<u> has every possibility that any crisis </u>==<u>may escalate</u>==<u> into a war</u>. ==<u>This</u>==<u> war may </u>==<u>result in an </u>==exchange of nuclear weapons (Ali and Sidhu, 2021). Regarding the conflict, both the states have a very particular mind-set that results in perceived or actual insecurities. Both view each other’s policies to maximize respective strategic gains in the region.

Pakistan reckons India as an occupying force in Kashmir that has committed horrific human rights violations besides unremittingly trashing UN resolutions on Kashmir. Pakistan deems the Kashmir issue as an unfinished agenda of sub-continent partition and proclaims that it is the “jugular vein” of Pakistan. Pakistan has remained baffled with grave reservations regarding increasing Indian inroads into Afghanistan before the fall of President Ashraf Ghani’s government in August 2021. The Pakistani anxiety was aroused from its reasonably good relations with Afghanistan’s monarchist, republican, and communist regimes preceding the Taliban’s first era (1996–2001). These anxieties continued to exist during Hamid Karzai and Ashraf Ghani (Constantino, 2020). This era (2001–2021) between the two Taliban regimes was the nadir of Afghanistan-Pakistan relations. The Indian support to Baloch insurgents in Balochistan province has perplexed Pakistan. The latter has voiced its concerns internationally by submitting dossiers to prove its claims against India at different forums, notably to UN General Secretary (Khetran, 2017). As far as India is concerned, it considers its conflict with Pakistan as a source of the impediment to its progress that has hindered India’s rise. It claims<u> that </u>Pakistan is harboring and sponsoring non-state actors<u> and using them to wage proxy and sub-conventional warfare in the region</u>, <u>especially </u>==<u>in </u>==Jammu==<u> and </u>==Kashmir. <u>India staunchly believes that such</u> a dangerous <u>policy</u> of Pakistan <u>is an </u>existential threat<u> to their national security and integrity</u>. Indian policy circles are dismayed that extending any concession to Pakistan by demonstrating any docility on Kashmir might have spillover effects on other parts of the country undergoing secessionist movements. <u>Consequently</u>, <u>India continues to trumpet the issue of terrorism and militancy in Kashmir by linking it with alleged Pakistani support</u>. India perceives that by pressurizing Pakistan at bilateral, regional, and international levels, it can deny any space to Pakistan on the Kashmir issue.
